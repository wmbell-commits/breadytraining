OSHA ETOOL + MEWP CLASSIFICATION

Scissor lifts and aerial lifts are not all treated the same.

OSHA’s Scaffolding eTool describes scissor lifts as mobile supported scaffold work platforms that move primarily straight up and down. OSHA’s aerial-lift guidance covers vehicle-mounted elevating devices such as extendable boom platforms, articulating boom platforms, aerial ladders and vertical towers. The applicable OSHA requirements can differ by equipment design and industry.

MEWP Group / Type note: the modern Group A / Group B and Type 1 / 2 / 3 language comes from the ANSI/SAIA A92 MEWP framework—not from an OSHA class system. B-Ready teaches operators to identify the category and operating characteristics of the actual machine they use.

Group A
The platform stays within the tipping lines in normal operation. Common example: many scissor lifts / vertical platforms.
Group B
The platform can extend beyond the tipping lines. Common example: articulating or telescopic boom lifts.
Type 1
Travel is permitted only with the MEWP in its stowed position.
Type 2
Travel with the work platform elevated is controlled from a point on the chassis.
Type 3
Travel with the work platform elevated is controlled from the work platform.

FALL PROTECTION + RESCUE

One rule does not fit every platform.

Fall-protection requirements depend on the equipment and applicable standard. OSHA emphasizes guardrail integrity for scissor lifts, while boom-type aerial lifts require appropriate personal fall protection under applicable OSHA requirements. Manufacturer instructions and the employer’s hazard assessment remain critical.

  • Guardrail condition and access gates
  • Approved anchorage points
  • Harness and lanyard / SRL selection where required
  • Restraint vs. arrest considerations
  • Platform-to-structure transfer restrictions
  • Emergency lowering controls
  • Ground-person responsibilities
  • Rescue plan for elevated workers
B-Ready emphasis: operators should know not only what fall-protection equipment is required, but why it is required, where it connects, what hazards it introduces and what the rescue plan is if the operator cannot lower the platform.
ADVANCED MEWP SYSTEMS

Modern lifts have more safety technology—and more to understand.

Current MEWPs may use electronic control modules, tilt sensors, load-sensing systems, secondary guarding, platform-position sensing, drive interlocks and sophisticated diagnostics. These systems can reduce risk, but operators still need to understand warnings, limitations and the correct response when a device activates.

SECONDARY GUARDINGCrush-protection bars, pressure-sensing devices and operator-presence systems designed to reduce entrapment risk.
TILT / LOAD SENSINGAlarms, motion restrictions or cutouts that respond to slope, overload or stability conditions.
FALL-PROTECTION SYSTEMSManufacturer-approved anchors, restraint systems, PFAS components and equipment-specific connection requirements.
ALARMS & INTERLOCKSAudible / visual warnings, pothole protection, gate switches, drive-height limits and function interlocks.
ELECTRONIC CONTROLSProportional joysticks, CAN-bus systems, fault codes, display panels and selectable operating modes.
TELEMATICS / ACCESS CONTROLOperator PINs, usage tracking, impact events, geofencing and digital pre-use inspection systems.

Training boundary: operator training explains system purpose, warnings, limits and safe response. Diagnosis, adjustment or bypass of safety devices belongs with qualified maintenance personnel and manufacturer procedures.

POWERED INDUSTRIAL TRUCKS

PIT / Forklift Safety Notes

Why is it called the stability triangle?

Many counterbalanced forklifts behave as a three-point suspension system: the two front-wheel contact points form the front of the triangle and the rear axle pivots about its center, creating the third support point. Stability depends on keeping the combined center of gravity inside that triangle.

See OSHA's Powered Industrial Trucks eTool in the resources section.

Training is truck-type and workplace specific.

A generic forklift card does not erase meaningful differences among Classes I–VII. Controls, visibility, operating characteristics, attachments and workplace hazards must match the equipment and conditions the employee actually operates in.

Electronic prechecks support the inspection—they do not replace it.

Telematics and digital checklists can document completion, restrict access and identify trends. Operators still need to understand what they are checking, recognize defects and follow the employer/manufacturer procedure when equipment must be removed from service.

Advanced guidance systems still require operator understanding.

Wire-, rail- or laser-guided narrow-aisle trucks can change steering, positioning and travel behavior. Training should address the installed system, alarms, limitations, emergency procedures and transitions to manual operation where applicable.

Forklift + personnel platform: this is not “just forklift work.”

A common training gap occurs when a forklift-trained employee is asked to elevate people in a work platform. Do not assume that placing a basket on forks automatically makes the operation acceptable. The truck, platform, securing method, capacity/markings, safeguards, controls and work procedure must all be appropriate to the actual application.

Start with the truck and platform manufacturers. Modifications or additions affecting capacity or safe operation must be addressed through applicable manufacturer approval and instructions.

Controls and safe descent at elevation matter. Some configurations may require platform-level control or power-shutoff provisions. Hydraulic/load-holding safeguards, emergency lowering, platform restraint, guardrails/fall protection and rated capacity also require attention. B-Ready treats this as a specialized personnel-elevation application rather than a generic forklift add-on.

AERIAL WORK PLATFORMS / MEWPS

AWP / MEWP Safety Notes

Can you exit an elevated platform?

Do not teach this as a blanket “yes.” Entering or exiting an elevated MEWP is a special-use situation that must be addressed through the applicable standard, manufacturer instructions, employer risk assessment and site procedure.

Pre-use means functional checks too.

A pre-use inspection is not just a walk-around. Operators should verify applicable controls, emergency functions and safety devices in accordance with the manufacturer and employer procedure before operation.

Know the rescue plan before going up.

Operators and designated ground personnel should know how emergency lowering works and what to do if the platform operator cannot lower the machine normally.

Machine type matters.

Scissor lifts and boom-supported MEWPs do not present identical hazards. Fall protection, stability, positioning, slope limits and operating procedures must match the equipment and manufacturer instructions.

OVERHEAD & GANTRY CRANES

Overhead Crane Safety Notes

The upper-limit device is not a routine operating stop.

Limit devices are safeguards. Normal lifting should be controlled with the operating control system and within the crane/hoist manufacturer's instructions rather than routinely driving the hoist into the upper limit.

Side pulling changes the loading.

Dragging or side pulling can introduce side loads, swing and unintended forces into the hoist, trolley, rigging and load. Keep the hoist line appropriately aligned with the load unless the equipment and procedure are specifically designed for another condition.

Granite and stone slabs create a crushing zone that is easy to underestimate.

OSHA has specific guidance addressing granite, marble and stone slabs. Workers can be exposed to catastrophic crushing hazards while separating, rigging, moving, loading and unloading slabs with overhead cranes and powered industrial trucks.

Think “fall shadow.” Keep people out of the area where a slab could land or topple. Use appropriate mechanical handling equipment and approved attachments, inspect slabs, racks and handling equipment, and use task-specific procedures.

This deserves task-specific instruction and controls—not merely a generic crane card or forklift card.

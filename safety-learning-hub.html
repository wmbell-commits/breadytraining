The rules people tend to miss.

Short practical reminders for operators, trainers and safety managers. These notes are learning aids—not a substitute for the applicable OSHA/MIOSHA rule, manufacturer instructions, workplace procedures or the complete ANSI/SAIA/ITSDF standard.

PIT / Forklift safety notes

Why is it called the stability triangle?

Many counterbalanced forklifts behave as a three-point suspension system: the two front-wheel contact points form the front of the triangle and the rear axle pivots about its center, creating the third support point. Stability depends on keeping the combined center of gravity inside that triangle.

Learn the concept from OSHA's Powered Industrial Trucks eTool below.

Training is workplace-specific.

Forklift qualification is more than a classroom card. OSHA's PIT rule requires formal instruction, practical training and evaluation, including workplace-related topics that apply to the operator's actual environment.

⚠ Forklift + personnel platform: this is not “just forklift work.”

A common gap occurs when a forklift-trained operator is asked to raise people in a work platform. Do not assume that putting a basket on the forks automatically makes the operation acceptable. The truck, platform, attachment, securing method, capacity/markings, controls and work procedure all have to be appropriate to the actual use, and the applicable requirements differ between general industry, construction and other regulated settings.

Start with the truck manufacturer. OSHA requires prior written manufacturer approval for modifications or additions that affect capacity or safe operation. OSHA also treats forklift work platforms as removable attachments, with attachment marking/capacity considerations. In construction, OSHA has stated that equipment not specifically designed by the manufacturer to support a scaffold platform cannot simply be used that way.

Controls at elevation are a major issue. OSHA interpretations addressing forklifts used to elevate personnel explain that certain configurations/equivalent high-lift order-picker uses require appropriate travel/power controls at platform level. Other OSHA rules can require an operator at the truck controls or means at the elevated platform to shut off power, depending on the equipment and industry. This is why B-Ready does not reduce the issue to one universal “basket rule.”

Hydraulic protection and descent behavior matter too. Do not assume a generic personnel basket cures a truck that was never designed/configured for personnel elevation. Verify the manufacturer's approved personnel-elevation configuration, hydraulic/load-holding safeguards, emergency lowering/power-shutoff provisions, platform restraint, guardrails/fall protection, capacity and operating instructions for the specific truck/platform combination.

AWP / MEWP safety notes

Can you exit an elevated platform?

Do not teach this as a blanket “yes.” Exiting or entering an elevated MEWP is a special-use situation that must be addressed through the applicable standard, manufacturer instructions, employer risk assessment and site procedure. The normal expectation is to remain within the platform and use the access system as intended.

Pre-use means functional checks too.

A pre-use inspection is not just a walk-around. Operators should verify applicable controls, emergency functions and safety devices in accordance with the manufacturer and employer procedure before operation. If an upper-limit or other limiting device is part of the required functional check for that machine, verify it before use.

Know the rescue plan before going up.

Operators and ground personnel should know how emergency lowering works and what to do if the platform operator cannot lower the machine normally.

Machine type matters.

Scissor lifts and boom-supported MEWPs do not present identical hazards. Fall protection, stability, positioning, slope limits and operating procedures must match the equipment and manufacturer instructions.

Overhead crane + stone slab handling

⚠ Granite and stone slabs create a crushing zone that is easy to underestimate.

OSHA has a specific Safety and Health Information Bulletin addressing granite, marble and stone slabs. Slabs can weigh hundreds to thousands of pounds, and workers have been killed or seriously injured while separating, rigging, moving, loading and unloading them with overhead cranes and powered industrial trucks.

Think “fall shadow.” Keep people out of the area where a slab could land or topple. OSHA recommends walking at the end of the slab rather than alongside its face, using appropriate mechanical handling equipment and approved attachments, inspecting slabs/racks/handling equipment, and using tag lines where appropriate to control suspended slabs. The attachment method, clamp location, stone condition and number of slabs being moved all matter.

This deserves its own task-specific procedure and training—not merely a generic crane card or forklift card.
